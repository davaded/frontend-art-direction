# Geometry And Edge Language

Use this guide when a surface feels square, rigid, over-divided, or mechanically boxed. The default posture is **soft structural geometry** unless the product, inspected reference, or explicit creative direction calls for precision, brutalism, industrial sharpness, or a spreadsheet-like grid.

Soft does not mean wrapping every section in a rounded card. It means the edge hierarchy, separation method, and transitions between regions feel deliberate rather than assembled from nested rectangles.

## Edge Hierarchy

Give different jobs different geometry:

1. **Page field**: usually open and borderless. Use spacing, crop, background tone, or an object silhouette to establish the field.
2. **Dominant stage or media frame**: the softest large contour, commonly `24-48px` or a shape derived from the product/reference.
3. **Secondary panel, sheet, or grouped region**: a smaller related curve, commonly `14-28px`.
4. **Controls and inputs**: compact tactile curves, commonly `8-16px`, with hit area and focus treatment taking priority.
5. **Pills**: only compact navigation, segmented state, status, or a deliberately capsule-shaped action.

Do not apply these numbers mechanically. Preserve an inspected reference or project-owned `DESIGN.md`, but record why a sharper or softer family is appropriate.

## Separation Ladder

Before drawing a line, try these in order:

1. spacing and alignment;
2. tonal or material shift;
3. inset, crop, overlap, or depth change;
4. controlled shadow or edge highlight;
5. border around a real object boundary;
6. divider only when it improves scanning or comparison.

Avoid nested border boxes, full-width rules between every section, and multiple 1px lines meeting at hard right angles. A layout may be rectilinear without feeling sharp when large fields are open, intersections have breathing room, and the dominant object has a softer contour.

## Line Policy

- Prefer short, low-contrast, local separators over page-wide rules.
- Give decorative or navigational paths rounded caps, softened turns, or a deliberate curve when the path itself carries character.
- Do not use a line merely because two things are adjacent; let spacing and hierarchy separate them first.
- In tables, use row rhythm, selection tone, and column alignment before a full cell grid. Horizontal rules are usually enough.
- Keep focus rings and safety boundaries explicit even when decorative dividers are removed.

## Sharp Exceptions

Sharper geometry is valid when it belongs to the product or direction:

- spreadsheets, code editors, engineering and measurement tools;
- brutalist, technical, archival, or print-grid art direction;
- industrial objects whose real silhouette is sharp;
- safety, selection, focus, and data boundaries that must remain exact;
- an inspected reference or project-owned design language that deliberately uses hard edges.

The exception should be localized and intentional. Do not let a precise table force the whole application shell, navigation, media, dialogs, and empty states into the same sharp geometry.

## Render Proof

Review desktop and mobile screenshots with color and content partially ignored:

- Trace the outer silhouette: does it read as one composition or nested rectangles?
- Count full-width borders and hard 90-degree intersections; remove those without an information job.
- Check the corner hierarchy: can users distinguish stage, panel, control, and status by geometry?
- Blur or thumbnail the screenshot: do spacing and tonal fields still group the page without divider scaffolding?
- Verify one dense or non-default state so soft geometry does not weaken scanning, focus, or selection.

When a reference is named, compare the edge hierarchy and separation method at the same viewport. Match the relationship, not a copied radius value.
